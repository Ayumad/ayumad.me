I've been into audio for a while now, and over time I've had the opportunity to own, borrow, and audition a pretty wide range of headphones, as well as put together a couple of speaker systems. Those experiences have helped me figure out what I actually enjoy listening to and what I want from my setup.

My actual introduction to audio was pretty modest. Before getting into any of this, I mostly used random Bluetooth headphones. My first more deliberate setup was a pair of Superlux HD 668Bs paired with a $20 audio interface.

I quickly moved on to a Beyerdynamic DT 770 Pro that I bought on Marketplace. They were over 20 years old and had suffered some pretty intense use before I got them, but they were my first step toward something a little more serious.

Eventually, I picked up a JDS Labs EL DAC II and a first-generation Atom amplifier. That combination served as my main DAC and amp stack for a long time, through several headphone purchases. Around that period, I also bought the HIFIMAN Edition XS, which blew away everything I had owned beforehand.

The XS was one of the first headphones I really enjoyed, and it became a familiar reference for a lot of what I tried afterward. Even now, after spending time with considerably more expensive headphones, I still think of it as my baseline for good audio and impressive soundstage.

Not every purchase worked out as well. I also owned a pair of the original Meze 99 Classics, which I found utterly underwhelming. Compared with everything else I owned at the time, they felt like a downgrade.

On the other hand, I really enjoyed the Sennheiser HD 6XX, which eventually led me to the HD 660S2 and a very different sort of listening experience from what I enjoyed about the XS.

Something I've come to appreciate through all of this is that headphones don't necessarily get better in every aspect as you spend more money. I've listened to headphones that are technically impressive but ultimately don't do much for me, and I've found others with obvious tradeoffs that offer a presentation I really enjoy.

I also don't necessarily want all my headphones to sound the same. I think there's value in having different sound signatures that complement different genres, recordings, and listening situations. Some of my favorite comparisons have involved finding out that a quality I enjoy on one recording becomes something I dislike on another.

I wanted to put that journey into words, from the headphones that first got me interested to the setup I enjoy using today.

## The Listening Session That Made Me Fall in Love With Audio

One experience that really pushed me further into audio was visiting an audio store fairly early in my journey. I already owned the HD 6XX, and I think this was slightly before I bought the Edition XS, although I don't remember the exact timing.

I auditioned a variety of headphones, but the pair that stood out most was the Focal Utopia 2022. At the time, I found them quite literally perfect.

They had an incredibly effortless presentation, and I couldn't identify anything about the sound that I wanted to change. The bass was tight and controlled, the mids sounded accurate and textured, and the treble was generally unoffensive in the best possible way.

I loved how everything came together. There wasn't a particular part of the presentation that distracted me or felt like something I had to accept in exchange for what the headphones did well.

The entire setup was far more expensive than anything I had listened to beforehand. Even the source and amplification stack cost several thousand dollars, so this was a fairly substantial jump from the equipment I knew at the time.

I've since listened to a much wider variety of headphones and speaker systems, and I've become considerably more experienced and critical. I have a better understanding of what I enjoy, what I'm listening for, and which tradeoffs matter to me. That first experience with the Utopia happened before I'd developed much of that perspective, but it remains a particularly magical listening session.

It was also my first complete listen to *Demon Days* by Gorillaz. I still don't think I've had a more ideal album listening experience than hearing it that way for the first time.

I can attribute a lot of what pushed me deeper into this hobby to that afternoon. I already had an interest in better audio, but that session gave me an experience I wanted to spend more time exploring.

## Audeze LCD-X — My Favorite Headphones, Easily

The LCD-X are easily my most engaging and favorite headphones that I own.

I think they deliver significant dynamic range, and I love the slam of the bass as well as the depth of textures they can reproduce. There's a physicality to their presentation that I haven't really found replicated by most of the other headphones I've tried.

What really stands out to me, though, is the bass extension and texture.

I've realized that I heavily enjoy subbass, and more importantly, subbass texture. The LCD-X do an incredible job of reproducing those really low frequencies in a way that feels both visceral and controlled. Bass has weight, but there's also a depth to it where you can make out the individual textures and variations within a sound.

That becomes especially interesting when there are overlapping bass sounds in a recording. I enjoy being able to focus on one layer and follow what it's doing underneath everything else, while still getting a satisfying sense of weight from the whole presentation.

For example, I've recently been borrowing the HIFIMAN Isvarna, which actually feels like it has more bass quantity and a stronger initial thump than the LCD-X. However, the LCD-X feels more fully extended, with bass that seems to get stronger the lower you go rather than drawing most of my attention to the region above deep subbass.

That isn't to say the LCD-X universally does bass better. On some tracks, I actually prefer the Isvarna's presentation, and the comparison has become more interesting as I've spent time with both.

Ultimately, the LCD-X is what I reach for when I want to be fully engaged with the music. It isn't necessarily the widest or most natural-sounding headphone I've tried, but there's something about its combination of dynamics, bass extension, and texture that makes it particularly satisfying.

I also listen to mine without EQ. I'm open to experimenting with it, but I've never really felt the need to fundamentally change their sound.

## Sennheiser HD 660S2 — My Favorite Way to Listen to Rock and Shoegaze

The HD 660S2 occupy a very different role in my collection.

I use them primarily for rock or shoegaze-adjacent genres where melting textures together in a satisfying way is most valuable, and I almost always use them with my Fosi Audio GR70 tube amplifier.

Before the 660S2, I had an HD 6XX that I really enjoyed. Moving to the 660S2 wasn't an improvement in every regard. I found the vocals a little weaker, and I missed some of the particular warmth I liked from the 6XX.

At the same time, the 660S2 had more texture in the midrange and a slightly warmer, more bass-forward overall presentation that I preferred for the rock-adjacent genres I mostly used it for.

Paired with the GR70, this is probably one of my favorite ways to listen to music involving heavily distorted guitars, layered instrumentation, and dense atmospheric production.

You lose details, especially in the treble, and also some of the subbass. But the textures in the mids, especially in songs with heavy guitar, things like shoegaze or fuzzy stuff, just sound incredible.

I don't think maximum separation and clarity are always the most desirable qualities. Sometimes having every instrument distinctly separated can take away from the overall presentation of a song, especially when I enjoy hearing it as a cohesive wall of sound.

The 660S2 with tubes seems to do an excellent job of blending those textures together without making the music completely indistinct. It's a warm, intimate, somewhat relaxed presentation that I find incredibly satisfying for the right genres.

I wouldn't choose this setup for music where deep subbass or precise separation is the priority, but that's also not what I keep it around for. With the music I use the 660S2 for, I hear the tradeoffs and still prefer the result.

## HIFIMAN Edition XS — My Baseline for Good Audio

The Edition XS was one of my first high-end headphones, and it was also one of the first that I really enjoyed.

Since then, I've tried a pretty wide variety of headphones, including several much more expensive HIFIMANs, but I still think the Edition XS does a remarkable job of demonstrating what makes planar magnetic headphones interesting.

It has an impressive soundstage, good detail retrieval, and a generally spacious, airy presentation. At this point, it almost serves as a baseline for good audio in my collection.

When I listen to something new, the Edition XS gives me a familiar reference for what a capable open-back planar sounds like.

I don't find it nearly as engaging as the LCD-X, and the HD 660S2 has largely taken over when I want something warmer and more intimate, but the Edition XS still does a great job of presenting music with a sense of space.

It also holds a somewhat sentimental role as one of the first headphones that really got me interested in higher-end audio.

Eventually, I intend to replace it with something like an Arya Organic or HE1000se. I'd like something that builds on the spacious HIFIMAN presentation while improving on its technical abilities and ideally offering something more engaging.

Having the XS around gives that comparison some perspective. I'm already familiar with a headphone that does soundstage well, so I'm interested in what else an upgrade adds to the experience.

## Sennheiser HD 490 Pro — Comfort and Versatility Above Everything

The HD 490 Pro is a somewhat interesting headphone in my collection because I don't find it particularly impressive in any single aspect of sound quality.

But I also find it incredibly difficult to identify any actual faults with it.

It has a generally balanced and versatile presentation that works well across a wide variety of music, and it doesn't really do anything that bothers me.

More importantly, its comfort is easily my favorite out of all my headphones that I not only own, but also have ever tried or worn.

That's a pretty significant achievement on its own.

There are plenty of times when I don't want to sit down and critically listen to music. I might be working at my desk, playing games, or just listening casually for an extended period. For those situations, the HD 490 Pro is an incredibly easy choice.

I can appreciate the sound of the LCD-X more and still prefer wearing the 490 Pro for a long session. Once I'm actually using a headphone for hours, comfort becomes a substantial part of how much I enjoy the overall experience.

It's a very competent headphone that's remarkably comfortable and easy to live with, even if it doesn't deliver the same immediate sense of excitement as some of my other headphones.

## Focal Azurys — A Closed Back I Actually Enjoy

The Azurys fills the closed-back role in my collection.

I don't find it better than any of my open backs per se, but I enjoy it enough to keep it around and think it's a very strong closed-back option.

One thing I particularly appreciate is its bass texture. It has a nice sense of body and warmth while still maintaining a reasonably clean and detailed presentation.

The biggest reason to own it, though, is practicality. Sometimes I want a more isolated listening experience without sacrificing too much of what makes my open-back headphones enjoyable, and the Azurys does that well.

It's not something I usually reach for when I have the freedom to listen to my LCD-X or 660S2, but it fits a role that neither of those headphones can really fulfill.

## Focal Clear OG — Why I Sold Them

I previously owned the Focal Clear OG, and my experience with them was somewhat disappointing.

I'd heard a lot about how dynamic and engaging they were supposed to be, particularly in terms of their punch and macrodynamics. I expected something that would give me a very immediate, involving listening experience.

But I honestly found them somewhat flat and unexciting.

Despite their reputation, I didn't really get the sense of dynamics I was expecting, and overall I found them boring and not especially engaging.

I don't think they were bad headphones. I could appreciate their technical performance, but their presentation didn't do much for me.

A reputation for dynamics doesn't necessarily mean a headphone will sound engaging to me. The LCD-X delivers a combination of slam and depth of texture that I find immediately satisfying, and I didn't get a comparable sense of enjoyment from the Clear.

I ultimately sold them because they weren't giving me an experience I wanted to keep returning to.

I'm still interested in trying the Focal Elex, particularly because its tuning is supposedly a little more energetic, but I wouldn't assume I'd prefer it. My experience with the Clear makes it something I'd want to hear for myself.

## Headphones I've Been Borrowing and Auditioning

I've also had the opportunity to spend some time with several HIFIMANs that I don't own. These have been especially interesting because they've helped me better understand what I enjoy about different planar presentations.

Borrowing them has also let me make more useful comparisons with my own headphones. A first impression can be interesting, but being able to return to the LCD-X or Edition XS and listen to the same recording makes the differences easier to describe.

### HIFIMAN Arya Unveiled — Naturalness and Openness

I'm currently borrowing the Arya Unveiled, and they sound incredible.

What stands out most is how incredibly natural and open they sound.

It's not necessarily game-changing in terms of soundstage or airiness compared with what I've already experienced from other HIFIMAN headphones, but the presentation is just very pleasant.

There seems to be an effortlessness to the way they reproduce music. Nothing feels particularly forced or exaggerated, and I find the overall sound very easy to appreciate.

It's a different kind of enjoyment from what I get out of the LCD-X. Where the LCD-X impresses me with physicality and texture, the Arya Unveiled impresses me with its naturalness and sense of openness.

I can appreciate the clarity and separation without feeling that those qualities are being made the entire point of the presentation. That's part of what has made it interesting as a possible reference for an Edition XS replacement.

That said, I do find the Unveiled design somewhat annoying.

While I think the exposed-driver concept is cool, I feel apprehensive about not storing them carefully, even with the protective covers. It's an additional consideration that I don't have with my other headphones.

I'm used to being able to put a headphone down without giving it quite so much thought. With the Unveiled, the exposed drivers stay in the back of my mind.

For something I'd potentially want to use every day, that matters. I think they sound incredible, but I haven't entirely decided how much that additional inconvenience would affect my experience of owning them.

### HIFIMAN Isvarna — A Surprisingly Interesting Hybrid

I'm also currently borrowing the HIFIMAN Isvarna, and in some ways I've found it even more interesting than the Arya Unveiled.

The Isvarna uses a hybrid configuration with a dynamic driver handling the bass and planar drivers handling the mids and highs.

I generally prefer planar bass, but this headphone has opened my ears to being impressed with dynamic-driver bass. The mids and highs coming from the planar drivers are very representative of what I think planars excel at, which is precision and clarity that feels effortless.

The bass is where things get particularly interesting.

Compared with my LCD-X, the Isvarna definitely feels like it has more bass quantity. However, it doesn't seem to focus as much on deep subbass extension.

Instead, it feels like there's a bump somewhere around the 50–100 Hz region, although that's just an approximation. What I'm trying to describe is an emphasis in the region above deep subbass, extending toward the lower midrange.

It does that transition from upper bass to lower midrange incredibly well. There's a stronger initial thump and a longer perceived decay, giving bass notes a lot of body and presence.

With the LCD-X, the bass feels more fully extended and stronger the lower you go. Initially, I also thought it was clearly a step above in texture and how visceral certain sounds felt.

Then I started finding recordings where I preferred the Isvarna.

One example is **"Angel" by Massive Attack**.

Around 18–20 seconds into the song, a second, deeper and cleaner bassline enters underneath the distorted bass that's already playing. I've been paying particular attention to the texture of that second bassline: the rounded, vibrating quality of it and how clearly I can follow it beneath the other sound.

Initially, I thought the Isvarna was noticeably better at reproducing it. The texture seemed easier to discern, while on the LCD-X it sounded comparatively quieter and was harder to focus on.

I later realized I wasn't matching the volume correctly, since I was swapping between the balanced output for the Isvarna and the 6.35 mm output for the LCD-X on my K13 R2R.

After accounting for that, I could hear the texture very well on both, but I still think the Isvarna barely edges it out.

On the other hand, with **"Fill the Void" by Salami Rose Joe Louis**, the Isvarna's bass feels strong but less controlled and a bit bloaty.

It has body, but instead of being nice, it feels like it gives the song a constant bulbous feeling that just muddies the mix. The LCD-X handles that presentation more convincingly to my ears.

I initially wondered whether this might be an electronic-versus-physical-bass thing, but I haven't isolated what's responsible. It could have more to do with the frequency balance and character of the bass in each recording.

What I find interesting is that a quality I enjoy on one track becomes excessive on another. The Isvarna's fuller, more lingering presentation can make a particular bass sound especially satisfying, but I don't always want that same fullness carried through the rest of the recording.

It has also made me more cautious about attributing the whole difference to driver technology. I'm hearing two complete headphones, and the Isvarna has already given me examples where I prefer its bass despite my general preference for the LCD-X.

The Isvarna is definitely no slouch, and I think it's a very interesting headphone. The LCD-X still has a combination of subbass extension, visceral texture, and consistency that I prefer overall, but I enjoy having the opportunity to hear where that preference changes.

### HIFIMAN Edition XV — An Alternative to the Traditional HIFIMAN Sound

I previously borrowed the Edition XV, and it stood out to me as an alternative tuning to HIFIMAN's traditional house sound.

Compared with the Edition XS, the XV has a slightly darker presentation. I still think it has decent detail retrieval; it just isn't as forward with it.

What I particularly enjoyed was how well that tuning complemented more rock-focused genres.

The Edition XV gives up some of that traditional HIFIMAN treble-forward presentation in favor of something a little warmer and more relaxed. I actually find that a worthwhile tradeoff for certain music.

I can enjoy what the XS does with space and clarity while also appreciating a headphone that brings those qualities forward a little less. With some of the music I enjoy through the 660S2, that different balance makes sense to me.

In that regard, I think the XV complements the Edition XS rather than simply replacing it.

### HIFIMAN Ananda Unveiled and Other Auditions

I've also spent some time with the Ananda Unveiled, along with a variety of other HIFIMAN headphones.

In general, I find HIFIMAN particularly good at delivering detail retrieval, clarity, and soundstage at their respective price points. That's part of what initially made the Edition XS so impressive to me, and it's something I still appreciate when trying their other headphones.

The Ananda Unveiled stays fairly bright in the treble, though, and I'd personally be interested in looking at some sort of Arya instead.

I also listened to the original Aune AR5000 a while ago, though not the Mk2, and thought they were good but not particularly special. They had a slight warm and dark tilt but were still generally enjoyable.

## Wireless Headphones — Convenience Still Matters

I also own a few wireless headphones and earbuds.

None of these are my preferred way of listening when I have access to my desktop setup, but I use them often enough that they're still a meaningful part of my collection.

### AirPods Max

I like my AirPods Max primarily for convenience.

I don't think they sound better than any of my dedicated audiophile headphones, but they're enjoyable enough for everyday use. One thing I particularly appreciate is their subbass performance.

They have a satisfying low-end presence, which is probably the part of their sound I enjoy most. Given how much I value subbass in my other headphones, that makes them easy to appreciate.

I don't usually reach for them when I want to sit down and focus on the finer details of a recording, but for general listening they're perfectly enjoyable.

### Sennheiser Momentum 4

I find the Momentum 4 roughly on par with the AirPods Max overall, although they have some differences in presentation.

The Momentum 4 sounds a little more natural to me, while the AirPods Max has the advantage in subbass.

I don't think either is universally better, and neither replaces my open-back headphones. They're options for situations where wireless convenience is a substantial part of what I need from the headphone.

### AirPods Pro 3

I currently use the AirPods Pro 3, having previously owned the AirPods Pro 2.

Interestingly, I actually thought the Pro 2 sounded better. However, I found them less comfortable, and they had a tendency to fall out of my ears more easily.

The Pro 3 works better for me from a comfort and fit perspective, which ultimately makes it more practical.

I can prefer the sound of the older pair and still be happier using the newer one. The difference in fit matters quite a bit when I'm actually using them.

## My IEM Collection — Where I Struggle the Most With Treble

I also own several IEMs, including the Moondrop x Crinacle Dusk, CrinEar Daybreak, Truthear x Crinacle Zero:Red, and B-Media Chorus.

My experience with IEMs has been somewhat complicated.

For the most part, I find them incredibly treble-forward. Almost all of the IEMs I own are either teetering on the brink of sibilance or are entirely too sibilant for general listening.

And it isn't necessarily just vocal sibilance. I find that almost anything toward the upper range of the frequency spectrum can become unpleasantly prominent.

This is interesting because I don't have the same problem with over-ear headphones, even ones that have a reputation for being relatively bright. I can enjoy the openness of the Arya Unveiled and the presentation of the Edition XS without experiencing that same persistent issue.

### CrinEar Daybreak

The Daybreak is what I'd consider my baseline for a good IEM.

I enjoy it, and I think it offers a solid level of performance, but it still doesn't entirely avoid the high-frequency presentation that I tend to struggle with.

It's useful as a reference point, particularly when comparing it with more expensive IEMs.

### Moondrop x Crinacle Dusk

The Dusk is a good step up from the Daybreak, especially when used with an Android phone.

Interestingly, I find that it sounds smoother on Android, whereas on iOS it feels sharper and less pleasant. This is all with the default DSP cable tuning.

I'm not entirely sure why I hear that difference. It could be related to the playback chain, volume, or some other interaction, but it's been a consistent enough subjective impression to affect which setup I enjoy using.

I think the Dusk is technically impressive and clearly capable, but the treble can still limit how much I enjoy it.

### Truthear Zero:Red and B-Media Chorus

I also keep the Zero:Red and B-Media Chorus in my collection.

They're useful points of comparison, especially given the different approaches to tuning available at relatively accessible prices. However, neither has really solved my broader issue with IEM treble.

### Softears RSV Mk2 — The Exception

One IEM I briefly auditioned that really stood out to me was the Softears RSV Mk2.

It was the only IEM I've tried where I didn't have the same issue with excessive treble. I found it enjoyable throughout the frequency spectrum.

That was honestly pretty refreshing. I could enjoy the overall presentation without the upper frequencies continually drawing my attention in an unpleasant way.

It was only a brief audition, so I'd want more time with it before forming the sort of detailed opinion I have about my own headphones. Even so, it left a strong enough impression to become one of my most useful references for future IEM listening.

I'm not necessarily looking for something dark or lacking detail. I'd like something that maintains clarity without making the upper frequencies feel disproportionately prominent, and the RSV Mk2 was the most promising example I've heard so far.

## My DAC and Amplifier Setup

```text
Creekwood (Windows)  →  USB  →  FiiO K13 R2R  →  headphones
                                     │
                                     └── RCA ──→ Fosi GR70  →  660S2
```

Most of my critical listening happens at my desktop PC, Creekwood.

I primarily use Tidal in exclusive mode, with lossless or Max quality audio. My main DAC and amplifier is the FiiO K13 R2R, which I often use in NOS and D2D mode.

I also have the Fosi Audio GR70 as a tube amplifier option, which I especially enjoy with the HD 660S2.

I've compared the K13 R2R against the Topping DX5 MkII and ultimately chose the FiiO. I found it a touch warmer, and I also just liked the way it looked on my desk more.

Something I've learned is that, given adequate power, I generally can't discern differences between good solid-state amplifiers very easily. I don't see much value in continuously upgrading DACs and amplifiers when the differences I'm hearing are often relatively minor compared with switching headphones.

The old JDS Labs stack served me well for a long time, and I'm similarly satisfied with the K13 now. I don't feel that the headphones I've been trying have automatically created a need to replace it.

The GR70 is a different story because I specifically enjoy the coloration it introduces with certain headphones and genres.

I don't think it makes everything technically better. There are situations where I can hear that it sacrifices some clarity and bass performance, but I like what it does to the midrange, especially with the 660S2.

That pairing gives me a clear reason to own a second amplifier. I can hear the change, I enjoy it with particular music, and I can use the K13 directly when I want its presentation instead.

I'm somewhat interested in trying an OTL tube amplifier eventually, something like a Bottlehead Crack with Speedball, particularly with the Sennheisers. I'm also interested in the possibility of a higher-end Topping amplifier at some point.

Neither is much of a priority, though. I'm pretty satisfied with my current setup.

## My Approach to EQ

I currently don't use EQ with my headphones, although I'm open to it.

Part of the reason is that I've always found it somewhat difficult to approach. I often can't hear small differences, while bigger differences feel like deliberate coloration that shouldn't necessarily be there.

I also appreciate the individual characteristics of different headphones and don't feel a strong need to EQ everything toward the same target.

For example, I like the LCD-X because of how it presents bass and texture, and I like the 660S2 because of the way it blends sounds together. Those differences are part of why I own both.

I realize that I already enjoy a deliberately changed presentation when I use the GR70 with the 660S2.

I'm open to experimenting with EQ in a similar spirit, but I think I'd find it easier to approach through a specific adjustment with a clear purpose.

If there's something about a headphone that bothers me, I can try changing that and decide whether I prefer the result. That feels easier to evaluate than applying a whole correction profile and trying to understand several changes at once.

I'm not opposed to making small adjustments to address particular shortcomings. I just haven't felt much need to change the fundamental presentation of the headphones I already enjoy.

## My Speaker Setups — Another Part of the Same Journey

My interest in audio also expanded into speakers, and both of my systems developed gradually alongside the headphones.

At my desk, I started with a Logitech Z623. Eventually, a monitor upgrade forced me to move away from a conventional audio jack and toward HDMI ARC, which led me to build my current setup.

That consists of a WiiM Ultra, Fosi ZA3, and a pair of KEF Q150s. I added an SVS SB-1000 Pro a couple of months later.

My living room went through its own progression. I started with random soundbars before moving to another pair of KEF Q150s. I eventually added a KEF Kube 12b subwoofer and later replaced the Q150s with Q500 floorstanders.

That system currently runs from a Yamaha receiver. I also intend to add another WiiM Ultra eventually so I can use its room EQ features to get the existing speakers and subwoofer better tuned.

## Where I Go From Here

At this point, I think I'm approaching a pause in my audio journey.

I'm always interested in listening to new things and having new experiences, and I'm very grateful for everything I've had the opportunity to hear so far. But aside from an upgraded pair of HIFIMANs and possibly a Softears RSV Mk2, I think I'm getting close to the limit of what I can reasonably purchase for myself and find worthwhile improvements.

For what I've spent overall, I feel like I've put together a pretty well-rounded setup. I have headphones that offer different presentations I enjoy, speaker systems I'm happy with, and sources that do what I need. There are still things I'd like to improve, but I don't feel like there are substantial gaps I need to keep filling.

Beyond those last couple of changes, most of the purchases that interest me start costing a thousand or several thousand dollars. I'm just not able to sustain that at this stage of my life. I expect this collection will stay with me, with only slight revisions, for several years, and I'm pretty comfortable with that.

Eventually, I would love to put together a summit-fi setup. The Audeze LCD-4 and several ZMF headphones interest me, along with potentially some higher-end HIFIMANs. I have loaner tours coming up for the HE1000 Unveiled and Susvara Unveiled, so I'll have an opportunity to spend time with those and see what I think.

I'm looking forward to hearing them, even if owning something at that level is a much more distant possibility. Borrowing headphones has already given me some of the most interesting experiences I've had in this hobby, and I'd like to continue doing that when I have the opportunity.

Similar reasons apply to my speaker choices. I think my systems deliver incredible price to performance, while still being very much the tip of the iceberg in terms of what's available. There's plenty more I'd enjoy exploring eventually, but I'm incredibly satisfied with where I've gotten so far.

I'm also especially grateful to be part of the Bay Audio Enclave. It's introduced me to several interesting people, given me opportunities to try a variety of headphones, and provided a real community to discuss and interact with around this hobby.

Having people to share impressions with and hear different perspectives from has become a substantial part of what I enjoy about audio.
